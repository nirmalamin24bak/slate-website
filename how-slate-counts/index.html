How Slate counts calories

Updated 13 October 2026.

Every number on Slate starts from a home recipe in grams. Each ingredient's calories, protein, carbs and fat come from the Indian Food Composition Tables (IFCT 2017), and the dish is then divided into the portions people eat: a katori, a roti, a plate.

Where do the values come from?

  • Energy for each ingredient is IFCT 2017's own value, converted from kilojoules at 4.184 kJ per calorie.
  • Pure fats such as oil and ghee have no energy value in the IFCT tables Slate uses, so they use IFCT's fat factor of 37 kJ per gram, about 8.84 calories per gram.
  • Ingredients IFCT doesn't list use USDA FoodData Central or the maker's label. Every dish page names the source for each ingredient.

How does a recipe become a portion?

A dish is a home recipe written in raw grams, and adding up its ingredients gives the whole pot.

When a published cooked weight exists, the pot is divided by it to get calories per 100 g cooked, and a portion is its cooked weight. A katori is its volume in ml times the dish's density, so a 200 ml katori of thin dal weighs less than one of thick dal.

When there's no reliable cooked weight, a portion is a share of the recipe instead, such as one of four servings.

Calories are rounded to the nearest 5 and grams to the nearest whole number.

What about food from outside?

Restaurant and street versions usually use more oil, ghee or butter, and bigger servings. Slate shows them as a range: the fat in the recipe is multiplied up, the portion is scaled up, and anything extra a restaurant usually adds is counted on top. Each dish page shows the range and the reasons for it.

How are the numbers checked?

Each dish is compared with published figures, such as ICMR-NIN's food tables. When a credible source is more than 15% away from Slate's number, the dish page shows a range and explains the difference instead of a single number.

Why do other sites show bigger numbers?

Many calorie sites list one serving without saying how big it is, often a large bowl or a restaurant recipe. Slate names the portion next to every number, so a 200 ml katori of home dal and a restaurant bowl don't get mixed up.

What does the app add?

In the app you tell Slate the size of your katori and roti and how much oil you cook with, and it scales these numbers to your kitchen. AI only reads lines Slate doesn't recognise; it never produces a calorie number.
